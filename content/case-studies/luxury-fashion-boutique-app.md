**Role:** Lead Designer\
**Work:** Website and customer mobile app for a luxury fashion and collectibles brand, with e-commerce flows and a design system\
**Under NDA:** Retail and lifestyle (fashion), no client names or figures

Luxury customers do not shop the way most people shop online. They do not want to filter 400 products. They want to be recognized, to get advice, and to feel that someone is paying attention to them. A standard online store turns the customer into a search box. This brand built its stores on personal service, so the job was to bring that service onto a phone without it feeling like a chatbot.

## Start from the store visit, not the catalog

Most e-commerce projects begin with product listing pages. For this kind of brand it makes more sense to begin with the visit. In the requirements sessions, a few plain questions decided the first screens:

1. How does a customer arrange a visit?
2. Who greets them?
3. What happens when the staff member cannot answer a question?

The answers led to private appointments and staff chat. They were designed first, and the shopping, promos, loyalty and reservations came after, built around them.

> A good digital product does not copy a store's layout. It copies how the store behaves.

## The service features are the product

Booking a private store visit and talking to staff are the main product here, and the catalog supports them. The cost is schedule. The shopping screens come later, so the client sees the plain part last. That is a fair trade, because the experience the brand cares about most gets settled while there is still time to change it.

## Chat needs a way to escalate

A chat box with one staff member breaks the first time that person does not know the answer. If the customer then has to explain everything again to someone new, the service feels cheap very quickly. So chat was designed with escalation: a staff member can bring in a manager or another branch, and the customer keeps the same conversation.

Escalation brings a staff-side problem. Someone has to decide who can see what, and when a conversation can move. That is more design work than a simple chat needs, and it is worth it.

## Every customer feature needs a staff counterpart

A chat message only feels attentive if the person receiving it has the tools to answer well. So both sides of the system were designed together:

| Customer sees | Staff needs |
| --- | --- |
| Book a private appointment | A schedule they can manage and confirm |
| Chat with a person | An inbox, plus a way to hand over to a manager or branch |
| Reservations and loyalty | The same details on their side, to serve the customer in person |

One design system runs across the web and the app, so the brand looks the same on every screen. Designing for two sides and two platforms is where a system pays for itself.

## Raffles and auctions need their own flows

Limited items are sold differently from regular products. Raffles and auctions need clear steps for entering, bidding and seeing your status. Keeping them apart from normal purchases means the two never get confused, because people should always know whether they are buying now or taking part in something.

## Why the ending matters

The Peak-End Rule says people judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and by how it ends ([Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/peak-end-rule/)). In a boutique visit, those moments are usually human: a warm greeting, and a clear answer at the end. It works as a check on the flows. If the peak and the end of a flow are still just a form and a confirmation page, the flow has not got the boutique feel yet.

## What to check next

- **Staff testing early.** Sit with the people who will answer the chats and run the schedule, and watch them use the staff screens before launch.
- **Escalation rules in writing.** Agree with the client on who sees what before the screens are drawn.
- **Clear status for limited items.** Entering, waiting, bidding and results should each look different at a glance.

A boutique app works when the person on the other end can actually help, so that person's screens deserve as much care as the customer's.
