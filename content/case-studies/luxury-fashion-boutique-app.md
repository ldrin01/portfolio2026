## The problem

Luxury customers don't shop the way most people shop online. They don't want to filter 400 products. They want to be recognized, to be advised, and to feel that someone is paying attention to them.

A standard online store can't do that. It turns the customer into a search box. The client's stores were built on personal service, so the question was how to bring that service into a phone without it feeling like a chatbot.

## My role

I led the design from the first requirements session to developer handover. I worked with the system analysts on what staff and customers needed to do, then designed both sides: what the customer sees, and what the store staff use to serve them.

## Process

1. **Requirements first.** I started with the in-store experience, not the catalog: how a visit is arranged, who greets the customer, and what happens when a staff member can't answer a question.
2. **The service features before the shop.** I designed private appointments and staff chat first, then escalation to managers and other branches. Shopping, promos, loyalty, reservations, raffles and auctions were designed around them.
3. **Two sides of one system.** Every customer feature has a staff counterpart. A chat message only feels attentive if the person receiving it has the tools to answer well.
4. **One design system** across web and the app, so the brand's look stayed the same on every screen.

## Key decisions

### 1. Service features came before the catalog

Most e-commerce projects start with product listing pages. I started with booking a private visit and talking to staff. For this brand, those features are the product. The catalog supports them.

**Trade-off:** the shopping screens arrived later in the schedule, so the client saw the "boring" part last. The benefit was that the experience the brand cared about most was settled early.

### 2. Chat that can escalate

A single chat box with one staff member breaks down the first time they don't know the answer. I designed escalation so a staff member can bring in a manager or another branch without making the customer repeat themselves.

**Why it matters:** repeating yourself is the fastest way to make a premium service feel cheap.

**Trade-off:** escalation needs clear rules about who sees what, which meant more staff-side design than a simple chat would have needed.

### 3. Raffles and auctions as part of the shop

Collectibles are sold differently from clothes. Limited items go to raffles and auctions, so those needed their own flows (entering, bidding, seeing your status) that sit next to normal purchases without confusing them.

## Why this project matters to me

It was a reminder that a good digital product doesn't copy a store's layout, it copies its behavior. The most important screen in the whole project might be the one where a customer says "can I come in on Saturday?" and a person says yes.
