Most of a designer's day is spent in Figma, and it is still worth starting with a pen. It is the fastest way to find out which ideas are bad before time goes into them.

It is also the habit juniors resist the most at first, which is why it is worth explaining.

## Figma makes you commit too early

The moment you draw a rectangle in Figma, you pick a width, a radius, a color and a font. Each choice feels small. Together they pull your attention to polish, when the real question at the start is whether this is the right idea.

Bill Buxton's book *Sketching User Experiences* treats sketching and prototyping as two points on a line. Sketches belong to early exploration. They suggest and question. Prototypes come later and are there to refine and commit. Trouble starts when you skip straight to the second.

Buxton also says a sketch is powerful because it is ambiguous. A rough drawing leaves room for interpretation, and that invites other ideas. A polished screen looks finished, so people react to the details and ignore the concept.

> A sketch asks "what if?" A high-fidelity mockup says "here it is." Early on, you want the question.

## Making many ideas beats perfecting one

A useful study here is *Parallel Prototyping Leads to Better Design Results, More Divergence, and Increased Self-Efficacy*, by Steven Dow and colleagues. In an experiment with 33 participants, novice designers made web banner ads for a magazine. One group made several prototypes in parallel before getting feedback. The other group made one at a time and got critique after each.

The parallel group's ads did better on click-through data and expert ratings. Independent raters also found their designs more diverse, and the participants reported a bigger gain in confidence.

The takeaway is that exploring several directions side by side beats polishing one and iterating on it. Sketching is the cheapest way to get several directions.

## A simple way to sketch

The setup is A4 paper or a sketchbook, a fine black pen and a thicker marker. Skipping the pencil helps, because erasing turns sketching back into polishing.

1. **Write the question at the top.** For example, "How does a driver confirm a delivery?" or "What does a student see after logging in?" One question per sheet keeps the page focused.
2. **Crazy eights.** Fold the paper into eight panels and draw eight different ideas in eight minutes. Most will be bad, and that is fine.
3. **Pick two or three.** Circle the ideas worth exploring and write what is good about each.
4. **Sketch the flow, not only the screen.** Buxton points out that sketching an interaction is harder than sketching an object, because it has to show time. So draw a few screens in order with arrows, like a storyboard.
5. **Annotate.** Short notes on what is happening and what is still open.

Only after that does Figma open, usually with lo-fi wireframes instead of UI.

## Where it saves the most time

In agency work, sketching pays off in a few places.

- **Kickoff and discovery.** Sketching in a workshop with the client and the system analysts helps everyone agree on what is being built before anyone argues about colors.
- **Complex flows.** Mobile apps with several user roles get messy fast. A trucking app with separate user and driver sides is one example. An exam review app with practice tests and progress tracking is another. Paper makes the flow visible.
- **Odd interactions.** An interactive mall map or a personality quiz for a drinks brand has no standard pattern to copy.
- **Layout exploration.** Five homepage layouts on paper take minutes. In Figma they take an afternoon.

## Sketches are social

Buxton describes sketches as social objects, made to be shared and talked about. Developers and system analysts in the room spot technical problems early, and they often suggest simpler solutions.

A sketch also lowers the bar for everyone. A project manager who would never open Figma will still pick up a pen and draw what they mean. That is often the moment a vague requirement becomes clear.

## Common objections

**"I can't draw."** You don't need to. Use boxes, lines, squiggles for text, and a circle with an X for images. If someone can tell what the box is, it is good enough.

**"It's faster in Figma."** For one screen, maybe. For eight different ideas, no. The ideas you explore in Figma also tend to be small variations of the first one you drew.

**"Clients won't take it seriously."** Raw sketches are a thinking tool for the team, and they work best shown to clients only when you are sketching together in a workshop. The client usually sees the wireframe that came out of them.

**"We have AI now."** AI tools are useful, and I use them a lot, including Claude Skills I built for lo-fi wireframing. They produce options fast. Choosing between options still takes a clear question and your own judgment, and sketching is how you get both.

## Tips if you are starting out

- Set a timer. A short limit stops you from polishing.
- Use a thick pen. You can't add too much detail with it.
- Keep every sheet. Old sketches show why an idea was dropped.
- Take a photo and drop it into the Figma or FigJam file, so the thinking stays next to the design.
- Sketch the empty, error and loading states too. They are usually forgotten until QA.
