**Role:** Senior UI/UX Designer\
**Work:** Storefronts and shopping apps for retail and lifestyle brands in the Philippines, including a fashion retailer's website and customer mobile app\
**Under NDA:** Industry only, no client names or figures

Most e-commerce guides are written for a shopper who sits at a laptop, pays by credit card and never talks to anyone. That shopper exists here, but they are not the majority. The shopper to design for is on a phone, wants to pay when the parcel arrives, and sends a message to the seller before buying anything.

## The market lives on the phone

Some numbers to set the scene:

| What | Number | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Philippine digital economy, 2025 | About $36 billion GMV (projected), up 16% | e-Conomy SEA, via BitPinas |
| Video commerce growth | 35%, the fastest-growing pillar | e-Conomy SEA, via BitPinas |
| Internet users, January 2025 | 97.5 million | DataReportal |
| Social media user identities | 90.8 million, about 78% of the population | DataReportal |

Most people meet a brand in a feed, on a phone, between other things. So a good starting picture for any retail project is a mid-range phone, mobile data that is not always fast, and a customer who found the shop through a post or a live selling video, not a search.

## Three habits that most templates miss

Ready-made storefront themes are good at the basics. These three local habits are where they fall short.

1. **Cash on delivery (COD) is still normal.** Xendit's 2026 report says COD is 23% of Philippine e-commerce transaction value on its platform, the highest of the six Southeast Asian markets it surveyed. Among Filipino shoppers who dropped an order in the last three months, 41% said the missing preferred payment method was their main reason ([Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/10/20/cash-on-delivery-still-dominates-phl-online-shopping)).
2. **People ask before they buy.** "Available pa po?" is almost a checkout step by itself. Shoppers message the page to check sizes, stock, delivery areas, and whether the photo matches the item.
3. **Surprise costs lose the sale.** Baymard puts average cart abandonment at 70.22%. Of the people who leave for reasons other than browsing, 40% blame extra costs like shipping and fees, and 12% could not see the total up front ([Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)). When budgets are tight, a surprise fee hurts even more.

> A Filipino shopper's checkout does not start at the cart. It starts in the chat window, and it ends when the rider hands over the parcel.

## Designing for it

The best approach is to treat the whole trip as the product: discovery, chat, cart, payment and delivery.

### Phone layout first

Draw the phone screen before the desktop one, and let desktop be the expansion.

- Product cards need big tap targets, a bold price, and the main action within thumb reach.
- Images should be cropped for portrait screens, because that is where social traffic lands.
- Pages stay light: one hero moment, compressed images, and skeleton states so a slow connection still feels alive.

### Payment options beside the price

A small row of payment methods (COD, GCash, cards, bank transfer) works best on the product page, not only at checkout. It answers the question before it becomes a reason to leave.

COD needs extra care, because the buyer has to be ready when the rider comes:

- Say clearly if COD is available for the shopper's area.
- Show the exact amount to prepare, in large plain numbers, on the confirmation screen.
- Keep order tracking simple so they know when to expect the rider.

COD is a risk for the seller too. For first-time COD orders, a light confirmation step is often a better answer than removing the option.

### Chat is part of the flow

It is better to design for the urge to message than to fight it.

1. A "Message us about this item" button on the product page can send the product name, size and photo into the chat, so the shopper does not need to describe it.
2. Clear size guides and photos of real people answer the common questions before anyone types them.
3. A visible chat schedule means silence does not look like "out of stock."

Chat matters in apps as well as on websites. The fashion retailer's customer app, built for luxury customers, included staff chat for exactly this reason.

### Show the total early

An estimated delivery fee on the product page helps. So does a running total in the cart that already includes shipping once a location is set. Vouchers belong beside the total, so the effect is visible right away.

## Trade-offs

Nothing here is free. These are the three that matter most:

| Choice | What it gives | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer COD | Wider reach, more trust | Failed deliveries, cash reconciliation |
| Add chat buttons | Fewer abandoned questions | More work for a small support team |
| Rich visuals and motion | A stronger brand feel | Slow pages on mobile data |

A sensible default is to let the business decide on COD, make the page answer most questions so chat only handles the real edge cases, and keep one rich moment per page with everything else lean.

## What comes next

- **Video commerce:** short product clips and live-selling replays on the product page, linked to size and add-to-cart.
- **Real devices:** regular tests on mid-range Android phones over mobile data, not office Wi-Fi.
- **Smarter COD:** offer it freely to trusted repeat customers, and guide new ones toward QR Ph or e-wallets.
- **Taglish microcopy:** interface text that sounds like the brand's own chat replies, as long as it stays clear.

Filipino e-commerce is not behind. It is just different. Design for the phone, the chat and the cash, and the shop stops feeling like a foreign template.
