Taste helps in art direction, but on agency projects most of the job is decisions. Pick a direction, explain it well enough that the client agrees, and then make sure several designers produce work that looks like it came from one hand.

In traditional advertising, the [art director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_director) owns the visual side of a campaign. They usually work with a copywriter who owns the words, and a creative director oversees both. As Assistant Creative Director at a digital agency, my role has a similar shape, applied to websites and apps.

## A direction has to answer more than "what does it look like"

On a website or app, a direction should pin down six things:

- **Mood:** how it should feel. Calm and trustworthy for a bank, warm for a café, quiet for a hotel.
- **Color:** the main palette, the accent, the neutrals, and how much of each.
- **Type:** headline and body faces, the scale, and how expressive the headlines get.
- **Imagery:** photo style, crop and lighting, whether people appear, and whether there is illustration or 3D.
- **Shape and space:** corner radius, density, and how much whitespace.
- **Motion:** how things enter, how fast, and how playful.

Each of these ends up as a design decision in Figma. That is why art direction comes before the design system.

## Step one: words before pictures

Before collecting any images, agree on a short list of mood words with the client and the team. Nielsen Norman Group's mood board process starts the same way, with mood words and their synonyms, and those words become the foundation for the board ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mood-boards/)). A short list works best. Too few is vague, and too many starts to contradict itself.

The words come from the brief, the stakeholder interviews and any research. "Modern" and "clean" do not count, because every client says them. Better words rule something out, like "unhurried", "clinical" or "playful".

## Step two: mood boards

NN/g describes mood boards as collages that show the feelings or values a product should bring out. They are easy to understand even for people with little design experience, which is why they work with clients. Nobody needs design training to react to a board.

A simple way to build one:

1. Review what exists: brand guidelines, past campaigns, the current site. The client may already have something worth keeping.
2. Collect widely. Photography, packaging, interiors, editorial layouts and film stills all count, not only other websites.
3. Arrange with hierarchy: one or two hero images, grouped supporting pieces, and some room to breathe.
4. Make two or three boards, each one a clearly different direction.
5. Add a line or two per board on what it says and why.

Building the boards in Figma or FigJam, next to the design file, lets the team comment on them directly.

> A board full of competitor screenshots is a reference board. It pulls the conversation toward features, when the board should be about feeling.

Other common mistakes are mixing contradictory moods on one board, and boards where every image is the same size so nothing leads.

## Step three: a style tile and one real screen

After the client picks a direction, move closer to the product. First a style tile with colors, type, buttons, a card and an image treatment. Then one key screen, such as the homepage hero or the main dashboard.

This is where the direction meets reality. A palette that looked great on a board can fail a contrast check on a button. A headline face that looked elegant can fall apart at mobile sizes. It is better to find that out on one screen than on forty.

## Step four: turn the direction into rules

A direction that only lives in one person's head does not scale. Once the key screen is approved, it becomes:

- Variables for color, spacing and radius, so the palette is applied the same way everywhere.
- Text styles for the type scale.
- Components that carry the shape language.
- A short do and don't page for imagery, with examples of good and bad crops.

This is the link between art direction and the design system. A designer who joins halfway can open the file and see what "on direction" means.

## Keeping a team on direction

Most of the time goes into reviewing work against the direction, not creating it. These habits help:

- **Review early.** A quick look at a half-finished screen is easier to correct than a polished one.
- **Point to the rule.** "This image is too busy for the calm direction we agreed" lands better than "I don't like it."
- **Keep the board visible.** Pin the approved board at the top of the design file.
- **Let it change on purpose.** Directions often shift once real content arrives. When that happens, update the rules so everyone moves together.

## Where 3D and AI fit in

I also work in Blender. 3D can be part of a direction: product renders, hero visuals, or a consistent object style that photography cannot give you. The same rules apply, and the lighting and materials have to match the mood words.

I also build custom Claude Skills for design work, and one of them is for art direction. AI can help with exploring options and with writing, but the decisions stay with the people on the project.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Showing one direction.** The client has nothing to compare it to, so the feedback gets vague.
2. **Showing too many.** With more than three, the meeting turns into picking favorite images.
3. **Skipping the rationale.** A board without the "why" gets judged on personal taste.
4. **Not testing with real content.** A direction built on stock photos breaks when the client's own photos arrive.

Good art direction is mostly a clear agreement that the whole team can check their work against.
