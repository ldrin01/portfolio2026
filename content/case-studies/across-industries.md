**Role:** Senior UI/UX Designer\
**Work:** Websites and mobile apps for clients in the Philippines, Australia, Germany and the US\
**Under NDA:** Industries only: education, banking, food and beverage, hospitality, retail, legal, recruitment, healthcare, nonprofit, logistics, media and an industry association

At the agency I switch to a new industry every few weeks. In four years the work has covered university and exam review sites, a bank and a pawnshop and money changer, restaurant, ice cream, café and bakery brands, hotel groups, a fashion retailer, malls, a law firm, a job board, a pharmaceutical company, a child sponsorship page, a trucking company's apps and a news agency.

A fair question is whether that much range makes a designer shallow. The other side is that it teaches you quickly which parts of the job stay the same and which parts you have to learn again.

## The process stays, the context changes

The steps are about the same on every project: gather requirements with the client and the system analysts, map the users and their tasks, wireframe, design, hand over to developers, and check the build against the design. What changes inside those steps is the users, the rules, the vocabulary and what "trust" looks like.

## What carries over

### People expect familiar patterns

Jakob's Law says users "prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know" ([Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/jakobs-law/)). A hotel booking, a job application and a loan inquiry are different businesses, but they all use forms, steps, progress and a confirmation. When the patterns are ones people already know, they spend their attention on the decision and not on the interface.

### Show the real cost early

A cake order, a hotel night and a money changer's rate share one need: people want to know the real cost before they commit. Baymard's research says that 40% of shoppers who abandon a cart for reasons other than browsing blame extra costs, and 12% could not see the total up front ([Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)). The numbers come from online shopping, but the idea fits any page that asks for money or time.

### Phone first

For Philippine clients the phone comes first. DataReportal counted 97.5 million internet users in the country in January 2025, and 90.8 million social media user identities ([DataReportal](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2025-philippines)). Menus, mall maps and exam reviewers are things people use while they are on the move.

### Structure before visuals

Large sites depend on their structure. Grouping, labels and navigation come before colour and type, whether the site is a university, a news agency or a pharmaceutical range. Nielsen Norman Group's guidance on mega menus applies here: keep the options organised so people can scan them ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/)).

### A design system saves time

I build and maintain design systems and team templates at the agency. A base of components, spacing and type scales means a new industry starts well ahead of zero, and the saved time goes to the parts that are unique to the client.

> The patterns travel. The context does not.

## What does not carry over

### Tone and trust

Trust looks different in each industry:

| Industry | What usually matters |
| --- | --- |
| Banking and pawn services | Calm layouts, visible rates, clear requirements, no playful copy near money |
| Legal | Discretion, clear practice areas, an easy and private way to make contact |
| Healthcare and pharmaceuticals | Careful wording, content for patients kept apart from content for professionals, review steps |
| Nonprofit child sponsorship | Honest stories and a clear account of where the money goes |
| Food and beverage | Appetite and personality |

The wine and beer site I worked on had a personality quiz. That idea would make no sense on a bank site, and it suited a drinks brand.

### Users with different days

The trucking project had a customer app and a driver app for the same business, which means two sets of priorities. Customers want to book and track. Drivers need large buttons, few steps and screens that can be read at a glance. One design language can serve both, with a different layout for each person.

### Payment habits

Payment expectations change by market. In the Philippines, Xendit's 2026 report says cash on delivery is still 23% of e-commerce transaction value on its platform. Among Filipino shoppers who dropped an order in the last three months, 41% said a missing preferred payment method was their main reason ([Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/10/20/cash-on-delivery-still-dominates-phl-online-shopping)). For clients in Australia and Germany, the same habits cannot be assumed, so it is better to ask.

### Rules and reviews

Some industries have rules that shape the design before anything is drawn. Finance and healthcare content often needs approval steps. In the Philippines, online merchants and e-retailers also have duties under the Internet Transactions Act of 2023, including showing the prices of what they offer ([Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.com/ph/en/insights/corporate-secretarial-alerts/internet-transactions-act-of-2023)). It is easier to ask about compliance in the first meeting than in the last.

## How to get up to speed

1. Ask the client to walk through a real day, the actual workflow and not the brand deck.
2. Read the competitors and the regulators. Competitors show what users already know. Regulators show what is allowed.
3. Learn the vocabulary. Requirements use the client's terms and the screen uses the users' terms, and they are often different.
4. Use AI as a research assistant. I build custom Claude Skills for UX research, user journey documents and wireframing, and use AI tools to speed up the first draft of summaries and structures. The result still gets checked and shaped by a person.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | What it gives | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Range across industries | Fresh eyes, patterns from many fields | Less depth than a specialist in one field |
| Shared design system | Faster starts | Sites that can look alike |
| Short discovery phase | Fewer surprises later | Time in a tight agency schedule |

The lack of depth is easier to cover by asking more questions and leaning on the client's own experts. Keeping the structure shared and letting type, colour, imagery and motion carry each brand helps with the "look alike" problem. A short discovery phase is worth keeping even on small projects, because skipping it usually costs more later.

## Where it could go next

- **Industry playbooks:** a short internal guide for each industry with its key users, trust signals, rules and common mistakes.
- **A pattern library by task:** booking, applying, paying and tracking show up everywhere, so tested versions of each would speed up every project.
- **Tests across markets:** quick usability sessions with users in Australia and Germany, and not only a review of their competitors.

Working across industries makes you less attached to any one look and more attached to the basics: know the user, respect their time and be honest about cost.
